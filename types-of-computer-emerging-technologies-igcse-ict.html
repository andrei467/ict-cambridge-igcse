Types of Computer & Emerging Technologies

Not every computer looks the same, and not every task suits the same type of machine. This page compares desktop and mobile computers, then looks at two technologies reshaping how we use them: artificial intelligence and extended reality.

Desktop computers

A desktop computer is designed to stay in one place, such as on a desk. It traditionally has separate parts — a monitor, the computer itself, and a keyboard & mouse. Desktop computers are typically more powerful than mobile computers, and their parts are upgradable, so performance can be improved by replacing components.

Mobile computers

A mobile computer is designed to be portable — a laptop, smartphone or tablet. It has built-in components (screen, keyboard/trackpad or touchscreen). Mobile computers are typically less powerful than desktops, because of power constraints from their size and the need to preserve battery life. Their components are integrated, so they're not easily upgradable.

Uses of mobile computers

  • Office & business — remote access to office applications, email on the go, mobile payments, calendars
  • Education — e-books, educational apps, note-taking and research
  • Gaming & entertainment — mobile games, streaming, social media
  • Remotely controlled devices — smart home control, surveillance access, controlling drones
  • Other — sat nav, online banking, photos, translation

Artificial Intelligence (AI)

Artificial intelligence is a machine that can simulate intelligent behaviours similar to a human. An AI system can:

  • Learn — acquire new information
  • Decide — analyse and make choices
  • Act autonomously — take actions without human input

There are two main types of AI:

  • Weak AI (narrow AI) — designed to perform a specific task or set of tasks
  • Strong AI (artificial general intelligence, AGI) — designed to perform any intellectual task a human can do

Extended Reality (XR)

Extended reality blends the virtual and real worlds to create immersion. Its two popular forms are virtual reality and augmented reality.

Virtual reality (VR)

  • Tries to shut out the "real world" and immerse the user in a fully simulated environment
  • Blocks out the real world using a headset with a built-in 360° screen
  • Users often interact with the virtual world through motion tracking

Augmented reality (AR)

  • Overlays digital information or graphics onto the real world
  • Users are not fully immersed — they can still see and interact with what's in front of them
  • Viewed via a smartphone or headset

XR is used in gaming, education and training for more engaging experiences, and applied in architecture, medicine and retail for better visualisation — but it raises concerns about addiction and excessive screen time.
